
Unix-based systems are operating systems that are either **directly derived from Unix** or **heavily inspired by its design and philosophy**. Let’s break it down:

---

### **1. Definition**

A **Unix-based system** follows Unix principles like:

- Multiuser and multitasking
    
- Hierarchical file system
    
- Everything is a file
    
- Shell-based command execution
    

Some are certified as “Unix” (by The Open Group), some are just Unix-like.

---

### **2. Categories**

#### **A. Original/Certified Unix**

- Developed directly from the original Unix at Bell Labs.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - **AIX** – IBM’s Unix for servers
        
    - **HP-UX** – Hewlett-Packard Unix
        
    - **Solaris** – Sun Microsystems’ Unix
        

#### **B. Unix-like (inspired by Unix)**

- Not certified, but behaves similarly.
    
- **Examples:**
    
    - **Linux** (Ubuntu, Fedora, Debian) – Open-source, Unix-like kernel
        
    - **macOS** – Apple’s OS, Unix-certified under the hood
        
    - **BSD variants** (FreeBSD, OpenBSD, NetBSD) – direct descendants of the original Unix
        

---

### **3. Key Features They Share**

- Command-line interface (shell)
    
- File permissions and user management
    
- Multiuser multitasking
    
- Network-oriented design
    
- Portable across hardware platforms
    

---

### **4. Quick Analogy**

Think of Unix as the **original recipe**, and Unix-based systems as:

- **Certified chefs** (AIX, Solaris) who follow the recipe exactly
    
- **Creative cooks** (Linux, macOS) who tweak the recipe but keep the core taste
